Founder story

Why I kept uninstalling break apps until I painted one

My eyes hurt by 3pm most days. I knew the fix. Everyone knows the fix. I just never did it.

I write code for a living, so that is a lot of hours staring at text a foot from my face. I knew the fix. Everyone knows the fix. Every twenty minutes, look at something twenty feet away for twenty seconds. The 20-20-20 rule. It is free, it works, and I could recite it in my sleep.

I just never did it.

Not because I am lazy about my health. Because the rule asks you to interrupt yourself at the exact moment you are least able to. Deep in a function, the last thing my brain volunteers is to stop and stare out a window. So like a reasonable person, I did what you do: I got an app to remind me.

Then I got another one. Then another.

The graveyard of uninstalled apps

Here is the part every maker will recognize. I tried a handful of break reminders, and I quit every single one, usually within a week. One threw a full-screen block over my work in the middle of a meeting and could not be stopped, so I looked like I had frozen on a call. One buzzed a little box in the corner that I learned to swipe away without even reading. They were, to a one, annoying. Eventually I removed them all for my own sanity, and I was right back where I started: sore eyes, and a small graveyard of uninstalled icons.

I sat with that for a while, because it is a strange failure. The apps did exactly what they promised. They reminded me to take a break. And I turned them off anyway.

The thing I finally understood is that nobody quits a break app because it lacks features. You quit because the break itself is unpleasant. A reminder you are told to obey is a chore, and chores lose to deadlines every time. The apps were optimizing the wrong thing. They were trying to be better at interrupting me, when the real problem was that I did not want the thing they were interrupting me with.

So I flipped the question

Not "how do I force myself to take the break," but "what break would I actually want to take?"

I am a Mac developer and, it turns out, a frustrated painter. The answer I landed on was this: when it is time to rest, my whole screen should fade into a hand-painted scene. Not a dimmed window. Not a countdown. A quiet, living painting, a meadow at midday, a coast at dusk, rain on a window at night, that I look forward to for twenty seconds and then leave, eyes rested, a little calmer than before.

That is the entire app. It is called Eye Rest. It lives in your menu bar, it is native SwiftUI with no Electron so it stays tiny and fast, and it fades your Mac into one of six scenes chosen by the time of day for each 20-20-20 break. A gentle heads-up appears a few seconds before, so it never ambushes you mid-sentence, and if it is a bad moment you skip or snooze it for free. It quietly pauses itself during video calls and browser video, because I never wanted to relive the frozen-on-a-meeting thing.

I did not build the most configurable break app. LookAway already does that, and does it well. I built the one I personally stopped turning off, which for me is the only metric that ever mattered. The 20-20-20 rule only helps your eyes if you keep it, and you only keep it if the break is something you want.

The six scenes

One for each kind of afternoon. Your Mac fades into whichever fits the time of day.

My eyes still hurt some days. But now I actually look away. Because for the first time, I want to see what is on the other side of the timer.
